From my second year of university through my fourth, I was mentoring people. At least eight students a year, sometimes more. Nobody assigned me this. There was no program, no club with a name, no schedule. People just found out I could code and started showing up.

## No structure, just a laptop and whatever they needed

It was never one language or one course. By that point I already knew eight different programming languages, so when someone came to me it was based on whatever they were actually trying to learn, not whatever I felt like teaching that week. Someone needed help with a language for a class assignment, someone else wanted to understand something for a personal project, and I would sit with them on my own laptop and work through it.

Here is the part that only occurs to me looking back. I had no mentor of my own. I was mostly self-taught, still figuring out the things I did not know, while at the same time being the person other students trusted to explain code to them. Nobody was checking my work or correcting my blind spots. I was teaching from a few steps ahead, not from some position of finished expertise. It did not feel strange at the time. It just felt like helping people, the way anyone who happened to know the thing would.

## Four of them, specifically

Out of everyone I helped across those years, four are worth naming what happened to. All four are working engineers now.

One of them did not come from a computer background at all. He was in a technical school track for electrical wiring, building electrical systems, the physical side of how a structure gets powered. Nothing about his path pointed toward software. He is a contracted engineer today, working for a client I know.

Another is a software engineer at a payments aggregator. A third went further into AI and works as an AI engineer now. The last one is a software engineer at the same place I work.

## The point

I mentored those years because I could and because people asked, not to build a résumé line. I had no mentor of my own at the time, and four people I helped then are working engineers now. You do not need to have arrived somewhere yourself before you can help someone else get further along than they were.
